Project overview

From research to practice organ (some day)

Technical Readiness Levels (TRLs)

Preliminary Research: TRL 1-2

I first learned about virtual pipe organ (VPO) software a few years ago during the COVID pandemic lockdown when I stumbled upon Hauptwerk for the first time. Always late to the game, I was was surprised to learn that the software had been around for more than 15 years, but was disappointed to learn that it was no longer available for free – and I had just missed it! I am not opposed to paying for quality software, but it was all so new to me that I was not sure what it was, how it worked, or even what I needed to get involved. In short, I was not ready to pay a monthly or yearly subscription for it.

I’m a strong proponent of open source community-driven software development. Of course, this model serves a very different purpose than commercial models, so not every field of software is suitable for open source. But I was relieved to discover that there was indeed an open source option for VPO, GrandOrgue, and it had a substantial user community behind it by that time. What was missing, however, was a documentation landing page attached to or associated with the GitHub repository where one ends up from Google. So it took me many short chunks of free time here and there over the course of many months to slowly make sense of what GrandOrgue was and how I might be able to use it. Eventually, after a few years of curiosity marked by occasional bursts of research motivation, I concluded that the only way I was going to learn this at all was to dig in and give it a try myself. And, I decided, while I was at it, I’d document my experience to address my criticism of lacking documentation. A starting point, at least.

In technical research applications, this preliminary research brought me to Technical Readiness Level (TRL) 2.1

Proof-of-Concept: TRL 3-4

My proof-of-concept setup involved an old MIDI-equipped Casio keyboard from the 1990s. This keyboard served me quite well growing up, and has made many geographic moves with me over the years, but its sounds are far from realistic by modern technological standards, so I was looking to give it a new life. Because this was my first setup, it is detailed most extensively throughout these pages.

I considered the initial functioning setup using the default GrandOrgue VPO to be my proof-of-concept system for TRL 3. I learned a lot while configuring this initial setup. Among other things, once finally up and running, the audio samples from even the GrandOrgue default organ quickly reaffirmed my disappointment with the built-in keyboard organ options. But most importantly, it proved to me that a virtual pipe organ (VPO) was worth pursuing for a decent practice platform.

Evaluating the platform (TDL 4) involved downloading other suitable VPO samples and testing how they performed and sounded on my available hardware. It quickly became evident that my limitations were no longer technical but practical: I could configure the software to do almost anything, but my experience with it as an organ would be incomplete as long as I only had one keyboard and no pedalboard.

Field Application: TRL 5-6

A big question in my mind throughout all of this investigation, starting from the early days of my initial research, was whether or not I should look into acquiring an older MIDI-capable used digital organ (or figuring out how to convert an even older analog organ into a MIDI-ready instrument) with the goal of using it entirely with VPO software. In my opinion, only the most recent digital organs feature truly authentic sound sampling technology: Rodgers, to me, has been far more successful in pioneering authentic-sounding digital organs for far longer than Allen. I would be satisfied with even a 10-15 year old Rodgers instrument, but I find even Allen’s most recent sampling technology to be characteristically flat sounding and unrealistic. But most used organs available at any given time are more like 20-30 years old, at least, so regardless of brand, their sound leaves a lot to be desired by modern standards. Since I don’t have the money to buy a brand new or even recent-trade pre-owned Rodgers or Allen organ, I began wondering if an older instrument controlling VPO software would suit my needs. To truly answer this question, I needed to try it out on an actual organ – without having to buy one first.

Available to me at the church I play at is an Allen MDS-6 AK digital computer organ from 1994. The console is MIDI-equipped, although the organ itself does not have a MIDI expander installed. This turned out to not be as challenging as I originally feared. The hardest part was actually the portability: transporting all of the required hardware, accessing the MIDI and audio connectors inside the organ console (the organ has no external MIDI or audio ports), and getting everything positioned in a functional way. It works, but it’s not pretty, especially for a communal place of worship. It is not something I’d deploy as-is operationally, that much is certain.

Getting my newly-created rig connected to and working with this organ in the church achieved TRL 5; using it for regular practicing achieved TRL 6.

An operational VPO: TRL 7-9 (COMING SOON?)

The more I use this VPO, the more convinced I become that this will be the easiest and most cost-effective way to get an authentic sounding practice organ at home. I’m still in the market for a home organ, but I’ve now expanded my search to include older MIDI-ready consoles that I could use exclusively with this, or an upgraded equivalent, setup. I’m still nervous about converting an old analog organ to MIDI, at least by myself, but maybe I’ll end up trying it on a small cheap or free instrument, like an old Hammond, if I’m able to find one locally some day. In the meantime, I’ll be sticking with my Casio keyboard at home and, when I’m feeling ambitious, the church’s old Allen organ.
